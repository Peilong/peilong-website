*Part 9 of 9 in the series [AI at a Small College](/tags/ai-at-a-small-college/).*

At a small college, much of our time goes to work outside class: committee minutes, accreditation self-studies, program reviews, office emails and grant proposals. This is where AI's strengths (summarizing, organizing and drafting from material you supply) fit best, as long as a few rules are followed. This last post covers that work, then ends with the question this series has kept returning to: **is it actually saving time?**

## Meeting minutes: record only what may be recorded

Most video-meeting platforms (Zoom, Teams, Google Meet) can now produce transcripts and AI summaries. Colleges with their own servers can also transcribe on campus. Either way:

1. **Announce the recording and note everyone's consent in the minutes.** Some states require every participant's consent.
2. **Never record tenure, promotion, grievance, conduct, academic-integrity or executive-session deliberations.**
3. **Draft from the transcript:**

```text
You are the committee secretary. From this transcript, produce minutes with:
Attendees; for each agenda item, a neutral 2-5 bullet summary (no verbatim quotes, and no
attributing opinions to people unless it is a formal motion); Motions (mover, seconder,
vote exactly as stated); Decisions; Action items (owner, due date); Deferred items.
Mark anything uncertain [CHECK]. Add nothing that is not in the transcript.
```

4. **The secretary edits; the committee approves.**
5. **Delete the recording once the minutes are approved.** Write that into your committee's procedures.

## Accreditation: allowed, but you own the accuracy

Accreditors have caught up:

- **Middle States (MSCHE)** adopted a policy effective July 1, 2025 that allows AI to be used in preparing accreditation materials. It is explicit that institutions "are responsible for the accuracy and integrity of all materials".
- **SACSCOC** has published "AI Good Practices".
- **C-RAC,** the council of regional accreditors, issued a joint statement in October 2025.

A good use: put the standards and your evidence inventory (documents, reports, minutes) into a private notebook or project that only the steering committee can open, in a college-approved tool. Then ask:

```text
For Standard [number], criterion [number]: list every document in this collection that
provides evidence, with file name and page. Then list the kinds of evidence a reviewer
would expect that seem to be missing.
```

For program reviews, start from tables your institutional research office has already prepared. Ask the AI to list every number it used, then have a person check each one against the source table. Keep a simple log of where AI was used, with the section, the tool and who checked it. Accreditors increasingly ask.

## Office work: shared prompts and a policy helper

For registrar, business office, advising and admissions staff, two habits save the most time:

- **A shared prompt library.** Most licensed tools let a team save and share prompts. Write one per recurring task, such as replying to a late-registration request, explaining a hold or summarizing a policy change. Paste the official policy text into the prompt itself, and put the allowed data class in its first line ([Part 2](/post/ai-series-2-before-you-paste/)). Staff paste in the question with the name and ID removed.
- **A policy helper.** Build an assistant that answers only from your office's *public* policy pages and help articles. Instruct it to quote the policy and section, and to give the office's contact when the answer isn't there. Review its thumbs-down answers monthly. Because it uses only public information, it's the lowest-risk, highest-volume place to start.

Anything that looks up an individual student's record (holds, aid status) needs integration with student systems and a formal contract. That is a procurement project, not a quick win.

## Grants and letters: AI may polish, not originate

**NIH** now says it will not consider applications "substantially developed by AI" to be the applicants' original ideas. If substantial AI use is detected after an award, NIH may refer the case to its Office of Research Integrity, disallow costs or terminate the award. Since September 2025, NIH also limits each principal investigator to six applications per calendar year. Other funders are moving in similar directions, so check each one. Safe uses:

- turn the funding announcement into a checklist of every required section, page limit and format, with a backward schedule from the due date;
- find literature through databases, with every source opened and checked by you;
- copyedit text you wrote, and note the date and tool;
- check every citation's DOI before you use it.

Never paste collaborators' unpublished ideas, reviewers' summary statements or proposals under review into a tool your college hasn't approved.

**Recommendation letters:** use AI for structure and polish only. Get the student's written permission before including anything from their records, and in any tool not covered by a college contract, refer to the student by initials.

## Is it actually saving time? Measure minutes, not impressions

Every national figure on time saved is self-reported, and some careful studies found AI *slowing* people down. The only number you can trust is your own. Here is a method any department or office can run in a semester:

1. **Pick three to five repeatable tasks,** such as minutes for a one-hour meeting, a reply to a common student request, feedback on one assignment or correcting captions for a 50-minute lecture.
2. **Baseline:** for two to three weeks, log each task done without AI.
3. **Trial:** for four to six weeks, log the same tasks done with AI, including checking time.
4. **Decide:** keep a routine only if it is faster and no less accurate. Mark the rest "do not scale" and move on.

A shared spreadsheet is enough. For example:

| Date | Task | With AI? | Minutes (incl. checking) | Errors found | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 3 | Committee minutes (60-min meeting) | No | 55 | — | |
| Nov 17 | Committee minutes (60-min meeting) | Yes | 25 | 2 names wrong | Fixed in review |

## Making it stick at a small college

National surveys show the gap is support, not interest. Only 6% of faculty fully agree that their institution provides enough AI training. A small college can close that gap cheaply:

- **Join, don't build.** The Council of Independent Colleges' AI Ready network costs $2,500 per institution and includes year-long programming, guides and webinars.
- **One required hour.** A 60-minute session on the data rules (Part 2), required before anyone gets access to licensed AI tools.
- **Short role tracks** for faculty, office staff and institutional research.
- **A monthly show-and-tell** where colleagues demonstrate one routine that worked, with their time-log numbers.
- **A few "AI fellows."** Give three to five faculty or staff a course release or stipend for one semester to pilot a routine, measure it, and write a one-page case note for colleagues.
- **Include assignment redesign.** Tyton's 2026 survey found that faculty who build AI into their assignments report lighter workloads than faculty who ban it, so training should cover course design, not just tools.
